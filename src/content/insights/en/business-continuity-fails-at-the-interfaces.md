A continuity plan often describes a process from the perspective of its owner. It sets out actions, resources, roles and an intended recovery time. The document can be complete within that boundary while overlooking the points at which the process depends on another team's decision, data from a supplier or a transfer of work to the next function.

Delay and conflicting expectations tend to develop at these interfaces. Each party may be following its own plan correctly while the overall response remains uncoordinated. An assessment of readiness therefore needs to consider both the content of individual documents and the quality of the connections between them.

## Process boundaries reveal the real continuity map

A process has inputs, recipients and conditions outside its direct control. It needs information, infrastructure, a decision or a service supplied elsewhere in the organisation. Its output then becomes the starting point for another role. A plan focused only on the owner's activities can leave these relationships implicit.

A useful boundary analysis identifies what must be delivered, by whom, in which form and by what time. It should also establish how the recipient will recognise that the handover is complete. An instruction to “contact the operations team” describes neither a decision nor the expected outcome.

Boundaries also reveal different interpretations of time. One team may state that a service will be restored within several hours, while a dependent process needs the underlying data earlier to meet its own obligation. Both plans look sound in isolation, but their timing assumptions conflict.

## Dependencies need mutual agreement

### Inside the organisation

A list of dependencies prepared by the process owner represents only one side of each relationship. A team may regard an internal service as critical even though its provider does not know about that assessment or has a different recovery priority. Naming the system or function does not confirm readiness.

Owners of connected processes should agree the minimum level of service, the point at which it is needed, how a request will be raised and what happens under constrained availability. They also need to know who resolves a conflict when several processes require the same resource.

### A dependency may be a decision

Not every dependency is a system or physical resource. A process may be waiting for approval, legal judgement, a priority decision or authority to make an exception. If the decision maker is unavailable, a technically ready team still cannot proceed.

The plan should identify authority, deputies and the information needed for that decision. Without them, the continuity route ends at a request for approval whose subsequent path is undefined.

### The relationship with an external supplier

A contract, service commitment and contact details are important, but they do not describe the whole response to disruption. A supplier may be supporting several customers at once, working to different priorities or providing updates at a rhythm that does not meet the organisation's needs.

The plan needs clarity over who initiates contact, how impact is confirmed, which actions the supplier can take and which decisions remain with the organisation. It also needs an alternative when the supplier does not respond or cannot meet the assumed timeframe.

Assumptions about external partners should be confirmed periodically. A change in service model, contact roles or subcontracting chain can make an earlier response path ineffective even though the supplier's name in the plan remains current.

## A handover needs ownership on both sides

A transfer of responsibility is often written as a single action even though it is a short process. The transferring party prepares information and confirms its status. The receiving party assesses completeness, accepts the task and becomes accountable for the next stage.

Without explicit acknowledgement, the two sides may understand the point of transfer differently. One considers the matter closed while the other waits for further detail. The resulting gap is absent from the action record of either team.

### Define the minimum handover

Depending on the process, the receiving team may need the current status, decisions already made, open risks, actions in progress, deadlines and constraints. The format can remain simple as long as it helps the recipient understand what is being accepted and which decision will be needed next.

The plan should also cover a failure of the standard handover route. If the main channel is unavailable or the designated role does not respond, the alternative must lead to someone with genuine authority rather than another unmonitored contact point.

## Escalation thresholds should describe the point of action

Broad terms such as “serious disruption” or “significant impact” allow wide interpretation. An operational team may wait for the event to develop further while recipients of the process are already experiencing an impact that calls for coordination at a higher level.

A threshold may refer to the duration of unavailability, the number of affected sites, loss of a particular capability, danger to people, breach of an obligation or the need to choose between competing priorities. It should be recognisable by the roles that first observe the situation.

Escalation also needs a recipient and an intended result. Merely informing senior management does not show whether a decision is required on plan activation, resources, acceptance of risk or communication. Naming the purpose shortens the path from signal to action.

## Hidden assumptions determine whether the plan is workable

Plans frequently assume that key people, systems, data and work locations will be available without saying so. They may also assume that disruption affects only one area, staff can change working arrangements quickly and other functions have spare capacity to help.

Assumptions are necessary where every condition cannot be predicted. They should still be visible and connected to a decision point. When an assumption no longer holds, the team needs to know whether to change priority, move to an alternative, reduce the service level or escalate the risk.

A plan review should examine the conditions on which execution depends. Assumptions shared by several processes deserve particular attention. If multiple teams rely on the same specialists, alternate location or rapid supplier support, stated readiness may exceed actual availability.

## Exercise interfaces and assign shared improvements

An exercise confined to one department shows only part of the system. A dependency test should carry information, a decision or a service across the boundary of responsibility. The organisation can then see whether both sides understand the handover conditions, use aligned priorities and can resolve a conflict.

Observation should cover the time taken to recognise the dependency, the quality of information transferred, clarity of ownership and effectiveness of escalation. The outcome does not always require a larger plan. A concise agreement between teams, a shared threshold or a change in authority may provide the necessary improvement.

An action relating to an interface needs one agreed owner even when several functions contribute. Without a single point of accountability, each party may close its own part while the connection remains ineffective.

Organisational resilience becomes visible in the movement between processes. A continuity plan is credible when dependencies are mutually confirmed, handovers have a clear point and owner, and escalation leads to a defined decision. Completeness matters, but coherence at the boundaries determines whether the plan can be used during a real disruption.
