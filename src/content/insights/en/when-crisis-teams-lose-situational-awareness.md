A crisis team can have access to extensive reporting, live conversations and operational data while still lacking a coherent view of the event. Each participant sees a different part of the situation. Information arrives at different speeds, its status is often unclear and the urgency of a message begins to stand in for its significance.

Situational awareness does not depend on complete knowledge. That condition is rarely available during a live disruption. It is the team's ability to recognise what is known, what remains uncertain, which changes matter and which decisions can no longer wait. It relies on shared discipline rather than another communication channel.

## The operating picture must serve decisions

Information becomes valuable when it is related to the team's purpose. A technical report may be detailed and accurate, but the crisis team needs to understand which elements alter the assessment of impact, the urgency of action or the available decision options.

The team should connect three levels of understanding: the current state of the event, its plausible development and the consequences for the organisation. A record confined to what has already happened soon becomes an archive. A forecast without clearly stated assumptions can be mistaken for a confirmed outcome.

### Decision-relevant information

For each significant update, the team should establish whether it affects people's safety, continuity of critical activities, organisational obligations, communication or the point of escalation. If it affects none of these, it can remain within the supporting information function rather than occupying the attention of the full team.

This approach does not restrict access to data. It structures how data is used. Analysts and operational specialists can retain the complete material, while the decision forum receives a concise current picture with the consequences made explicit.

## Separate facts, assumptions and unconfirmed reports

Under time pressure, the boundaries between these categories can weaken. A report repeated in several conversations begins to sound reliable even when every speaker is relying on the same unverified source. A working assumption can remain in circulation after the conditions that supported it have changed.

Every important element of the operating picture should carry a visible status. A fact has been confirmed through an identified source. An assumption allows work to continue despite missing information and needs an owner and a review point. An unconfirmed report is a signal to verify, not a foundation for confident conclusions.

### Language that preserves the distinction

Briefing language matters. Phrases such as “we have confirmed”, “our current working assumption is” and “we have received an unconfirmed report” help the team judge the quality of the decision basis. Precise wording reduces the chance that confidence in delivery will be confused with confidence in the information.

Status must change as the event develops. When an assumption is confirmed or disproved, the update belongs in the shared picture. It cannot remain only in the memory of the person managing that line of inquiry.

## Maintain a common operating picture

A Common Operating Picture gives the whole team a working point of reference. It does not have to be a complex technology platform. A structured document, board or small set of screens can be sufficient if the material follows a consistent arrangement and the current version is unambiguous.

The picture should cover the current state, confirmed impacts, actions in progress, material risks, decisions required and unresolved questions. The time of the latest update and the owner of each area are equally important. Without them, the reader cannot tell whether the display is current or predates the latest change.

### Unresolved questions belong in the picture

A list of open questions can be as important as the list of confirmed facts. It shows where the team is operating with limited knowledge and which gaps may alter a decision. Each question needs someone responsible for finding an answer and a point at which the team will review it.

Questions do not all deserve equal priority. Those that could change the protection of people, the selected course of action, the scale of resources or the content of communication come first. Others can be deferred without losing control of the event.

## Decisions and information ownership

### The decision log

The operating picture describes the state of the response, while a decision log preserves its logic. It should show what was decided, when, by whom, on the basis of which information and which actions follow. A concise entry is adequate if it allows the reasoning to be reconstructed later.

The log prevents the team from repeatedly reopening settled issues without a new reason. It also makes it easier to change a decision when conditions move. The team can then identify which new information challenged the earlier assumption and what part of the response needs to be adjusted.

Decision records should be made during the meeting, not reconstructed from memory afterwards. The person documenting them needs to work alongside the discussion and confirm the wording of each conclusion. Actions, owners and deadlines then remain aligned with the decision maker's intent.

### The information owner

During a crisis, several functions may collect data at the same time. Without explicit ownership, an important issue can be watched by everyone and managed by no one. Information ownership covers collection, assessment of status, updating and presentation of consequences to the team.

The owner does not always need to be the most senior person. The role should have access to the right sources and the ability to maintain that part of the picture. A crisis team needs clarity over who is covering people, operations, technology, suppliers, legal concerns, communication and any other area material to the event.

Ownership also includes reporting the absence of information. An owner who cannot confirm a report should say so and set out the next attempt to verify it. Silence can otherwise be interpreted as an absence of concern.

## Use a briefing rhythm instead of a continuous stream

Passing on every new item immediately can create an impression of currency while making analysis and completion harder. Team members repeatedly interrupt their work to hear another fragment, and the picture changes without clear control points.

A defined briefing rhythm separates time for collection and verification from time for shared assessment. The frequency depends on the pace of the event. The next briefing should have a known time, a stable structure and a mechanism for convening earlier when a development requires an immediate decision.

### The discipline of a short briefing

A briefing can begin with changes since the previous control point, followed by impact, actions, unresolved questions and decisions. Each function reports only what changes the shared picture. Detailed working discussions can take place outside the main forum and return with an outcome.

This rhythm limits information overload without isolating the team from important signals. It also gives participants an opportunity to prepare a concise, considered update instead of narrating the process by which they reached it.

## The leader protects the quality of the picture

The crisis leader does not need to collect information personally. The leader is responsible for the conditions that keep the operating picture useful. That includes separating discussion of facts from the choice of action, asking about sources and confidence, and recognising when further delay in search of data has become a decision in its own right.

Several consistent habits support that discipline:

- name the decision the team currently needs,
- confirm the facts and assumptions on which it will rest,
- identify the information that could change the selected direction,
- assign an action owner and a review point,
- close lines of discussion that do not affect current priorities.

Situational awareness will always be incomplete and temporary. Its quality can be judged by whether participants understand the same state of the event, can name the most important gaps and know which decisions are due now. When those conditions are present, the team can act responsibly even while full certainty remains out of reach.
