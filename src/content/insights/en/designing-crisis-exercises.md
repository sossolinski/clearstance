The scenario attracts attention, but it should not be the starting point. Begin by deciding what the organisation needs to test and which decisions the exercise should bring into view.

## Begin with the objective

An exercise objective should be specific enough to make behaviours, information flows and decision quality observable. A broad intention to “test readiness” does not yet provide a useful basis for design.

Before writing the scenario, ask three questions:

1. Which roles and dependencies need to be tested?
2. What kinds of decisions should the team have to make?
3. What information will be needed to make those decisions possible?

### An example observation frame

| Area | Observation question |
| --- | --- |
| Escalation | Was the situation recognised and escalated at the right point? |
| Information | Did the team establish a shared operating picture? |
| Decisions | Was accountability for the decision clear? |
| Communication | Did the messages address the needs of their audiences? |

## The scenario should activate the team

A useful scenario is not a story with one correct answer. It creates conditions in which participants must organise incomplete information, recognise change and align on a direction of action.

> An exercise becomes valuable when it reveals how the organisation works, not only whether people know the documents.

### After the exercise

The debrief should connect observations to concrete changes. Findings can be organised around a simple frame:

- what supported effective action,
- what made coordination more difficult,
- which assumptions need to change,
- who owns the next action and by when.
