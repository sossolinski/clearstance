A crisis exercise is easily judged by its visible qualities: a realistic scenario, smooth delivery and engaged participants. These features may support the experience, but they do not determine its value. The organisation needs a credible observation of how it works under pressure.

An exercise that runs too smoothly can conceal important problems. Participants anticipate the next event, controllers help them through difficult moments and the closing discussion focuses on general impressions. The resulting narrative is coherent, yet it offers little evidence about how the team recognises change, escalates risk and makes decisions.

## Objectives define the boundaries of the scenario

Exercise design should begin with a clear account of what the organisation needs to observe. An objective to “test readiness” is too broad to guide the work. The design needs specific areas of interest, such as the point at which a crisis team is activated, the quality of the shared operating picture, a decision to protect people or coordination with a critical supplier.

Each objective should lead to observable behaviour and decisions. If escalation is under review, the scenario must create conditions in which participants can recognise a threshold and make a choice. Where the sequence automatically activates the highest response level, the exercise cannot show how the organisation assesses warning signs.

A limited set of objectives allows greater depth. A complex scenario involving many functions may appear ambitious, but the observation team may be unable to follow every dependency with care. Scope should reflect the available time, the seniority of participants and the capacity for analysis afterwards.

## Decisions reveal how the organisation works

Procedures provide an important reference, but locating the correct document says little about the quality of a response. The most demanding moments tend to sit where the procedure leaves a choice, information conflicts or several justified priorities compete.

Designers should identify the decisions that the exercise is intended to bring into view. For each one, they can define the information available, the person or forum with authority, the relevant timeframe and the consequences of delay. The purpose is to create conditions in which reasoning and accountability become visible, without preparing one correct answer.

### Leave room for a genuine choice

If every message directs participants towards the next point, they are following instructions embedded in the scenario. A useful exercise gives them room to set priorities, reject an immaterial line of inquiry or seek missing information.

The consequences of decisions should influence what follows. Within a prepared logic, controllers can adjust later information so that participants experience the effects of their chosen direction. This flexibility calls for careful preparation, but it gives the exercise greater credibility.

## Injects should create pressure without arbitrary noise

An inject has a defined function within the exercise. It can reveal a change, create the need for a decision, test an information route or show the consequence of an earlier action. The number of messages is not a measure of realism. A high volume of unrelated prompts can make the intended observation harder to see.

Each inject should connect to an objective and an anticipated response. The design team needs to know who will receive it, in what form, which part of the operating picture it changes and how control will respond if participants take a different route from the one anticipated.

### Use realistic information gaps

Incomplete information belongs in a crisis exercise, but it must still permit purposeful work. Participants need ways to ask questions, consult available sources and receive answers that are consistent with the simulated environment.

An information gap should reveal how the team manages uncertainty. If control repeatedly refuses answers without a reason, participants are reduced to guessing the designer's intention. If every answer arrives immediately, the exercise cannot show how they prioritise and act with limited knowledge.

## Control maintains the environment

The control team protects the coherence, pace and safety of the simulation. It should not become an additional adviser to the crisis team. A prompt given to preserve the planned sequence removes a difficulty that might otherwise deserve attention after the exercise.

The boundaries of assistance should be agreed in advance. Controllers may explain the rules, respond on behalf of simulated organisations and resolve technical issues. They should avoid suggesting decisions, reminding participants about overlooked roles or accelerating escalation simply because the scenario expects a later stage.

When participants choose an unexpected direction, controllers need to assess its credibility and relevance to the objectives. The sequence may be adapted, or a line of play may be closed when it no longer contributes. Forcing every decision back into the script can preserve fluency at the expense of useful observation.

## Observation requires a method

A note stating that “communication needs improvement” is too general to support action. Observers should work from questions derived from the objectives and record specific behaviours, moments and consequences. What happened should be distinguished from an interpretation of why it happened.

For a decision, observation might cover when the need was recognised, what information was available, whether the right roles contributed, how options were considered and how the resulting action was assigned. For information flow, useful detail includes the source, route, recipient's understanding and incorporation into the common operating picture.

Observers should not judge every difference against their personal preferred practice. The objectives, agreed plans and operational outcome provide the reference. A departure from procedure may show an error by participants, but it may also reveal that the document does not fit operating conditions.

## Findings need to become owned action

A hot wash immediately after the exercise captures fresh observations and reactions. It is useful first material, but it does not provide a complete analysis. Participants saw only part of the event, and highly visible moments may overshadow issues that developed outside the main forum.

The next stage should bring together observations, control records, decisions and perspectives from different roles. Findings then need to be assessed for significance. Some gaps could materially affect a live response, some may be isolated and others may indicate a structural problem in authority, plans or preparation.

An improvement action needs an owner, an intended result and a review date. A general commitment to update a procedure may be inadequate if the difficulty concerned authority, access to information or coordination across functions. Closure should confirm that the change has been implemented and understood by the people expected to use it.

A good exercise may run calmly or reveal serious difficulties. Its value lies in the quality of insight the organisation gains into its own performance and the discipline with which that insight becomes a practical improvement in readiness.
